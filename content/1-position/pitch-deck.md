# Build Your Pitch Deck

Create a concise pitch deck that forces strategic clarity — whether you're raising money or not.

## Why This Matters Even If You're Not Raising

Most founders think of pitch decks as something you build when you're raising money. This is wrong.

Building a pitch deck forces you to answer questions you might be avoiding:
- What is the size of the market you're going after?
- What's the unfair advantage that makes you likely to win?
- What does traction look like so far?
- What specifically does the money (or effort) go toward?

If any of these questions makes you uncomfortable, that discomfort is signal. Better to surface it now than after you've spent six months on marketing that doesn't work because the strategic story isn't clear.

A pitch deck also gives you:
- A one-slide summary to share with potential partners
- A framework for partner / integration conversations
- A reference document for your own roadmap decisions
- An asset for press coverage and media outreach

Build it today. You'll use it more than you expect.

---

## The 10-Slide Structure

This is the standard format that investors expect — and it's the right structure for clarity regardless of audience.

| Slide | Title | What It Answers |
|-------|-------|----------------|
| 1 | Problem | What specific problem exists and why does it matter? |
| 2 | Solution | How do you solve it? |
| 3 | Product | What does it actually look like / how does it work? |
| 4 | Market | How large is the opportunity? |
| 5 | Business Model | How do you make money? |
| 6 | Traction | What's happened so far? |
| 7 | Competition | How do you fit in the landscape? |
| 8 | Team | Why are you the people to build this? |
| 9 | Financials | What are the key numbers? |
| 10 | Ask | What do you need and what will it unlock? |

---

## Step 1: Write Your Slide Outlines

Don't start in slide software — start with text. Write the key claim for each slide in 1-3 sentences, then the supporting points.

```
Write a 10-slide pitch deck outline for [product].

Context:
- Product: [description]
- Market: [from market research brief]
- ICP: [primary ICP]
- Current traction: [what exists — beta users, signups, revenue]
- Business model: [pricing tiers, revenue model]
- Funding stage (if any): [pre-seed / seed / bootstrapped]
- Key differentiator: [from positioning]

For each slide:
1. Slide headline (the single claim that slide makes — 5-10 words)
2. 3-5 supporting bullet points
3. The one visual or data point that best supports this slide
4. What question this slide must answer for the investor/partner/reader

Make the narrative arc clear: Problem → Stakes → Solution → Proof → Opportunity → Team → Ask.
```

## Slide 1: Problem

The problem slide does one thing: make the audience feel the pain.

**What makes a strong problem slide:**
- Specific, not abstract ("Marketing teams spend 40% of their time on content production that still underperforms" — not "content marketing is inefficient")
- Quantified where possible (hours wasted, money lost, percentage of work that doesn't drive results)
- Relatable to your audience (if pitching to investors, connect to something they understand)

```
Write a problem slide for [product]'s pitch deck.

The specific problem: [detailed description]
Who has it: [ICP]
What it costs them: [time / money / stress / opportunity]
Current workarounds and why they fail: [list]

Write:
1. Slide headline (the problem in one sentence — bold claim)
2. 3 supporting statistics or data points
3. One vivid example (a story of a day in the life of someone with this problem)

Make it visceral. The audience should feel the problem, not just understand it.
```

---

## Slide 2: Solution

The solution slide shows the transformation — from the painful state on slide 1 to the better state your product enables.

**Structure:**
- One sentence: what you do
- Three key points: how you do it (outcomes, not features)
- Demo screenshot or product visual

**What to avoid:**
- Listing features instead of outcomes
- Being vague ("we use AI to make content better")
- Claiming too much ("completely automated marketing")

---

## Slide 4: Market — The SAM / SOM Problem

Most founders either (a) cite a huge TAM number that isn't credible or (b) cite a tiny niche that seems too small. The right answer is in the middle.

Use a three-layer market sizing:
- **TAM** (Total Addressable Market) — the whole pie
- **SAM** (Serviceable Addressable Market) — the segment you can actually reach
- **SOM** (Serviceable Obtainable Market) — what you can realistically capture in 3 years

```
Help me build market sizing for [product].

My target: [ICP description]
Geography: [US only / global / specific region]
Market definition: [from market research]

Calculate:
1. TAM: total market if everyone with this problem used a solution
2. SAM: the portion we can realistically serve given our go-to-market approach
3. SOM: our realistic 3-year capture based on comparable company growth benchmarks

Show the math. Use bottom-up methodology (number of target buyers × average contract value) as a sanity check against top-down (market reports × percentage).
```

---

## Slide 6: Traction — Prove Something Is Working

Traction is relative to stage. Pre-launch traction might be:
- Beta users actively using the product
- Waitlist signups with a high conversion rate
- LOIs from potential customers
- Social following growth
- Press or community mentions

Present whatever you have honestly. Investors understand that pre-seed companies have pre-revenue metrics. What they're looking for is evidence that you can get people to pay attention and take action.

```
Help me frame my traction for a pitch deck.

What I have:
[list everything — signups, beta users, revenue, engagement, press, social, community]

For each metric:
1. Put it in context (what does this compare to? what's the benchmark?)
2. Show the trend (if growing, show the growth rate)
3. Identify the most important "lead indicator" — the one metric that suggests future success

Write the traction slide narrative: [3-4 sentences that tell the story of our momentum]
```

## Building the Deck: Tool Options

Use [DeckChat.ai](https://deckchat.ai) to build your deck with AI:
- Upload your slide outline
- Generate slide designs
- Iterate on individual slides with chat

Use [STORYD.ai](https://storyd.ai) for data-driven slides:
- Best for the market size slide, financial projections, and traction charts
- Creates polished data visualizations that look investor-ready

**Quick assembly in Google Slides or Figma:**
If you need speed over polish, write your 10-slide outline, then build in Google Slides in one sitting. Use a clean template, minimal text per slide, and one strong visual per slide.

---

## Slide 10: The Ask

Be specific. "We're raising money to grow" is not an ask. "We're raising $500K to hire two engineers and fund 6 months of paid acquisition" is an ask.

Even if you're not raising, write a "what we need" slide:
- Specific partnerships you're looking for
- Customer pilot programs
- Strategic introductions to specific companies

```
Write a pitch deck ask slide for [product].

If raising: amount, use of funds (specific line items), milestones this unlocks
If not raising: specific partnerships, pilot customers, or strategic support needed

Write:
1. The headline ("We're raising $X to do Y" or "We're looking for X type of partners")
2. Specific use of funds or specific needs (3-5 bullets)
3. What success looks like 12 months from now (milestones)
4. Contact / next step CTA
```

## Deliverable

One markdown file: `pitch-deck-outline.md`

Include all 10 slides with:
- Slide headline
- Key points
- Recommended visual
- Speaker notes (what you say, not what's on the slide)

Then build the actual deck in [DeckChat](https://deckchat.ai) or [STORYD](https://storyd.ai).
